# The Heali launch that kept slipping

## Describe failing to meet a major deadline or KPI. Tell me about a commitment you missed, and how you rectified it.

At Heali we were supposed to launch. Every time the date came up we postponed it at the last minute, because we were missing a feature, or a button. That went on for two years. No single postponement looked like a mistake on the day — each one was a small, reasonable "this isn't ready yet" — and like a frog in a pot that comes to the boil slowly, I wasn't feeling it happen: we were feature creeping one defensible delay at a time, and I was the one signing off on them. We ended up with a Frankenstein app, every piece of it asked for by somebody and the whole worse than any of its parts, so we paid for those features twice: once to build them, and again in the time it took to prune them back out and align the product with its users. Rectifying it was removal — cutting the app back until it matched what people actually needed rather than everything they had mentioned.

## What did you learn from missing that deadline? What would you do differently?

Simplicity always wins, and I learned it the hard way, over two years and a launch date I kept moving. The lasting change was to how I take a request: it's less about what customers, or friends and family, tell you they want, and more about what they need. Those two sound identical while you're taking notes in the room, and they are not the same product. If you can see the difference, that's the difference between a standard TV remote — a button for every function anyone might want — and an Apple one.

## How do you tell a reasonable delay from feature creep?

I distrust the slow kind of failure now. A project that blows up in a quarter gets noticed and fixed; a project that slips two weeks at a time, with a good reason every time, can run for years before anyone names it. So I set the date and treat moving it as the exception that has to be argued for, rather than the default that needs a reason not to.
